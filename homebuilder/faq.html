COMMON QUESTIONS

Practical answers without unnecessary technical language

Construction can feel complicated when you are seeing the terms for the first time. These answers are written to help a non-technical property owner understand the important decisions.

Grey structure generally refers to the main structural and masonry stage of a building before most final finishes are installed. Depending on the agreed scope, it can include excavation, foundations, RCC work, brick masonry, roof slabs and related structural work. Turnkey construction goes much further: it coordinates the structural work plus building services and finishing such as flooring, paint, doors, sanitary fittings, electrical fixtures and other agreed items. The exact inclusions should always be written in the contract because contractors can define packages differently.

There is no single reliable price for every house because the total depends on covered area, architectural and structural design, soil and site conditions, basement requirements, material specifications, labour, finishing level and changing market prices. A useful estimate should therefore be based on the actual drawings, covered area and scope rather than a generic plot-size figure. If an online estimate is used, treat it as a planning figure rather than a final quotation.

The timeline depends on the size and design of the house, whether there is a basement, the construction scope, approvals, weather, material availability and the speed of decisions. Grey structure and complete turnkey construction also have very different timelines. A responsible contractor should provide a project schedule with stages and explain which factors can cause delays rather than promising an unrealistic fixed completion date.

Ask the contractor to explain exactly what is included, what is excluded, which materials or quality standards will be used, how payments are linked to work stages, who supervises the site, how variations are approved, how quality issues are handled and what the expected timeline is. Also review genuine previous projects and references where available. The cheapest quotation is not automatically the best value if it leaves important items undefined.

A Bill of Quantities, commonly called a BOQ, is a structured document that breaks a construction project into measurable items or work categories. It helps the owner and contractor understand what is being priced and makes quotations easier to compare. For a non-technical homeowner, the important point is that a clear BOQ reduces ambiguity: you can see what work is being allowed for, what quantity or specification is assumed, and which items are excluded or provisional.

Yes. Many property owners choose to complete the structural stage first and handle finishing later, either with the same contractor or a separate team. If you plan to split the work, make sure the grey-structure scope leaves the building ready for the next phase and that important interfaces—such as plumbing routes, electrical conduits, waterproofing and levels—are properly coordinated. Written handover information can make the transition much easier.

Cement, steel, bricks, labour, transport and finishing materials can change in price over time. That is why a website should not present an old rate as a permanent market price. A proper quotation should state its date, scope, material assumptions and validity period. When prices move significantly, the contract should explain how approved changes or variations are handled.

An overseas owner should make the scope, drawings, budget, payment stages, reporting method and decision-making process clear before work begins. Regular photographs or video updates can help, but they should support—not replace—proper site supervision and documentation. It is also useful to nominate a trusted local contact for urgent decisions and keep all approvals, quotations and payment records organized.

Covered area is the built-up floor area used for many construction estimates. It is not always the same as the plot size because setbacks, open areas, parking, terraces and bylaws affect how much can actually be constructed. For an accurate estimate, the contractor should work from the architectural drawings and actual covered-area calculations rather than assuming that every square foot of the plot will be built.

A good contract should clearly identify the project, scope of work, drawings, specifications, material standards, exclusions, price or pricing method, payment stages, timeline, responsibilities, variation procedure, quality expectations, site access, handover requirements and how disputes or changes will be handled. The more clearly these items are documented, the less room there is for misunderstandings later.

Ask for simple explanations of the drawings, scope, materials, stages, costs and risks. If a quotation is full of technical terms, ask the contractor to explain what each major item means and how it affects your house.

Do not compare only the price per square foot. Put the quotations side by side and compare covered area, structural scope, material brands or grades where specified, finishing standards, labour, electrical and plumbing scope, external works, taxes or fees, exclusions, payment stages, timeline and variation rules. A lower price may simply mean that more items have been excluded or assumed at a lower specification.
